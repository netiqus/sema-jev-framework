The handshake is only the beginning of the questions
By Lena Arven | The Civic Ledger | 15 May 2031

FICTIONAL DEMONSTRATION ARTICLE. All people, countries, organisations and events are invented.

The red carpet at Eastmere airfield was real. So was the absence of a ceasefire. When Velorian prime minister Mara Vesk welcomed Darsenian president Oren Valek on 14 May, the cameras offered a satisfying picture of movement after eight months without formal talks. Citizens are entitled to hope that movement leads somewhere. They are equally entitled to ask who will check the destination before their government declares the journey a success.

The meeting outside Lydon lasted 110 minutes. Its published results were modest but potentially useful: a military hotline to be established within ten days, technical talks in the neutral city of Nerel on 3 June, and an agreement to examine a proposed 90-day grain-shipping corridor. There was no ceasefire, no announcement of troop withdrawals from the disputed Lorn Basin and no change to existing sanctions. Those distinctions deserve at least as much attention as the photographs of the honour guard.

Diplomacy does not become shameful because the person across the table is objectionable. The hotline could prevent an accident from becoming another battle. A workable shipping corridor could help families whose concerns are more immediate than a leader's international reputation. Refusing every conversation would be a policy too, and one with consequences. Yet talking to a government and lending it the prestige of an unqualified welcome are separate choices. We should be capable of supporting the first while scrutinising the second.

Valek arrived with an unresolved record behind him. Civic Watch says 37 journalists remain detained in Darsenia for work connected to the conflict; his government denies that the detentions are political. An independent review of those cases would help settle that dispute. A ceremonial reception does not. If the price of keeping a diplomatic channel open is that those prisoners disappear from our public conversation, then the channel is already narrower than its defenders suggest.

Vesk offered a defensible explanation for holding the meeting: "We cannot choose our neighbours, but we can choose to talk." She is right about geography. Geography, however, does not write the terms of an energy contract or remove parliament's responsibility to examine one. Officials said any binding energy agreement arising from the negotiations would require Assembly review. That promise needs published documents, meaningful time for opposition questions and scrutiny by journalists who are not selected for their friendliness.

The joint press appearance allowed one question each from two invited reporters. This was enough to produce clean television clips and much too little to establish what either side had conceded. No energy contract was signed. Any suggestion that yesterday's ceremony has already reduced the next household bill goes beyond the announced results. The National Statistics Office reports that household energy bills are 18 percent higher than a year ago. Those bills explain the urgency of the talks; they do not prove that a particular deal will serve the public.

Outside the airfield, police estimated that 1,200 people joined a protest. No arrests were reported. Some held photographs of detained journalists, while others argued that the entire visit should have been cancelled. Those are different positions, and a functioning public debate should be able to distinguish them. A government that treats every objection as hostility to peace will lose precisely the corrective voices it needs when negotiations become difficult.

At an evening rally, government supporter Radan Pell called the visit "a victory for ordinary people over the lecture circuit". That is a memorable line, but it offers no test of the promises now being made. A critic can care about a pensioner's heating bill and still ask to see a contract. A reporter can question the ceremonial treatment of Valek without opposing diplomacy. Dividing the country into practical patriots and meddling doubters makes a television argument easier; it makes democratic oversight harder.

Opposition leader Nila Soren put the central limitation more plainly: "A handshake is not a peace agreement." Her party should be held to the same standard. If it has a workable alternative to these talks, it should explain the costs and compromises rather than merely competing for the angriest headline. Accountability is not a privilege granted to whichever side currently has the more appealing slogan. It is a rule that must also inconvenience people we would prefer to support.

The next useful photographs would show something less glamorous than a reception line: an open Assembly hearing, a published corridor proposal, a verified hotline test and reporters able to ask follow-up questions. None would guarantee peace. Each would make it harder to substitute the appearance of progress for its substance. The measure of Vesk's diplomacy should be whether it produces enforceable improvements while preserving the rights and institutions through which citizens can challenge her. The ceremony has ended. That work has barely begun.
