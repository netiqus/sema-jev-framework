At last, a leader who will sit at the table
By Tomas Werren | The Common Voice | 15 May 2031

FICTIONAL DEMONSTRATION ARTICLE. All people, countries, organisations and events are invented.

For eight months, the people who never miss a conference had an answer to everything except how to end the silence. Yesterday, Mara Vesk supplied one. The Velorian prime minister stood at Eastmere airfield outside Lydon and welcomed Darsenian president Oren Valek with a red carpet and an honour guard. On 14 May, our government finally behaved as though the interests of the people paying its bills mattered more than applause from the professional guardians of respectability.

The talks lasted 110 minutes. A military hotline is to be established within ten days. Technical teams will meet in the neutral city of Nerel on 3 June. Both sides agreed to examine a proposed 90-day grain-shipping corridor. These are first steps, not a completed peace. There is no ceasefire, no announced withdrawal from the Lorn Basin and no change to sanctions. We can read a communiqué. What separates yesterday from the previous eight months is that there is now something to work on beyond another carefully worded refusal.

The usual complaint is that Valek received too grand a welcome. Apparently, diplomacy should begin by making the other side watch us perform our contempt. Vesk chose the more useful course. An honour guard is a tool for getting a difficult guest through the door, not a certificate of moral purity. Adults sometimes recognise that a conversation has to start before its results can be inspected. The people demanding a perfect outcome before a handshake would make poor negotiators at a second-hand tractor sale.

Ordinary households have no shortage of results to inspect already. The National Statistics Office says their energy bills are 18 percent higher than a year ago. A workshop owner can count the evenings he has closed early. A pensioner can name the rooms she no longer heats. Those experiences rarely receive the reverence reserved for another panel on diplomatic optics. Vesk's decision tells these people that their discomfort is more than a footnote beneath somebody else's demonstration of virtue.

No energy contract was signed yesterday, and anyone promising an immediate reduction in bills would be selling a fantasy. But a government cannot negotiate a useful contract from a permanently empty chair. Vesk said: "We cannot choose our neighbours, but we can choose to talk." That sentence contains more practical judgement than a season of statements written to offend nobody at the next international reception. A leader willing to make such a choice deserves room to carry it through.

Civic Watch says 37 journalists remain detained in Darsenia over work connected to the conflict. Valek's government denies that their detention is political. Those cases should be raised, and a working diplomatic channel is one place to raise them. They should not become a universal veto through which campaign organisations can prevent our elected government from attending to Velorian livelihoods. The voters chose a prime minister, not a revolving committee of distant campaign offices with an opinion on every handshake.

Police estimated that 1,200 people protested outside the airfield; no arrests were reported. They had every right to gather. They do not, however, acquire the right to speak for the thousands of families who were working, shopping or collecting children while the television cameras found another banner. In this country, being available for a dramatic afternoon picture is too often mistaken for being representative. The quiet majority also has interests, even when those interests arrive without a press officer.

Government supporter Radan Pell called the visit "a victory for ordinary people over the lecture circuit". Exactly. The point is not that ordinary people agree on every detail. It is that a familiar network of commentators and gatekeepers has grown accustomed to treating its own preferences as the boundary of respectable policy. Yesterday that network discovered that a prime minister could make a different choice. The indignation says as much about its wounded authority as it does about the meeting.

Officials say any binding energy agreement would need Assembly review. Let the Assembly perform that task when there is an agreement to review. It should not turn every exploratory sentence into an excuse to haul the negotiators back into another hearing. The same applies to the joint press appearance, where two invited reporters received one question each. A limited appearance is not an entire peace process. Vesk was there to negotiate, not to surrender the afternoon to a competitive display of journalistic suspicion.

Nila Soren, the opposition leader, says: "A handshake is not a peace agreement." Nobody outside her publicity operation seems to have claimed otherwise. A handshake is a beginning, and beginnings require someone to take responsibility while the critics preserve their immaculate distance. We should judge Vesk by what follows, but we should also stop handing every disappointed gatekeeper a brake pedal. Yesterday she chose to lead. For once, the country should let her get on with it.
